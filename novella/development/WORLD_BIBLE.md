# World Bible: The Weight of Atlantis
## A Complete Setting Reference for HPMOR-Adjacent Historical Fantasy

*Created for the dual-protagonist novella exploring the origins of wizard-Muggle separation through the eyes of Arthur Pendragon (14-year-old rationalist king) and Merlin (the ancient wizard who discovered Atlantis's terrible truth)*

---

## 1. World Overview

### The Setting in Three Paragraphs

**The Weight of Atlantis** unfolds in the dying embers of Roman Britain, circa 475-477 CE. Rome withdrew its legions a generation ago, leaving the Romano-British population to fend off Saxon invasions, internal collapse, and the slow death of everything the Empire built: roads, law, aqueducts, literacy, trade networks, medicine. Into this chaos rises Arthur—a peasant boy of fourteen who pulled a sword from stone and now bears the weight of a crown no one believes he deserves. He's trying to hold civilization together through systematic thinking and careful observation, qualities that will later be called rationalism but which he experiences simply as *paying attention to what works*.

Magic exists in this era, but it is rare, fragmented, and hidden. There are no schools, no formal training, no magical community. Hedge-witches practice in villages, passing down charms from mother to daughter. Cunning-folk sell protective amulets and healing potions. The occasional wizard wanders the land, hoarding knowledge because that's what keeps them valuable—and alive. Most people have never witnessed real magic. Those who have keep quiet about it. The Church views it with deep suspicion, somewhere between devil's work and heresy. Magic-users survive by staying invisible.

But beneath this surface scarcity lies a truth so terrible that it has shaped all of magical history: there was once a civilization—Atlantis, though that name means nothing to anyone in this era—that achieved what Arthur dreams of. They systematized magic. They democratized power. They built a shining future where knowledge was free and justice was real. And they destroyed themselves so completely that they were erased not just from existence but from the causal timeline itself. Only one artifact survived: the Mirror of Erised, lodged in the wound of reality like shrapnel. And only one man knows the truth: Merlin, who bears the weight of preventing it from happening again.

### Core Premise

A rationalist king who believes all knowledge should be free confronts a wizard who knows that some truths are too dangerous to share. Together, they discover that restriction can be an act of mercy, that justice and survival sometimes conflict, and that the loneliest burden is knowledge too terrible to speak aloud.

## 3. The Physical World

### Primary Setting: Arthur's Court

**Not Yet Camelot**: Arthur's seat of power is not a shining castle but a repurposed Roman fortress in western Britain. The location is deliberately unspecified (the historical record is sparse and contradictory), allowing for narrative flexibility while maintaining period authenticity.

**The Great Hall**:
- **Visual**: Stone walls built by Roman engineers, now cracking and water-stained. Timber roof added later, poorly fitted. High narrow windows that let in more cold than light. The floor is fitted stone, uneven now, with gaps where frost has heaved the slabs.
- **Auditory**: Wind whistling through gaps in shutters. The crackle and pop of the central hearth. Echoes—voices carry strangely in the vaulted space. Outside: horses in the courtyard, the clang of the smithy, dogs barking, the cry of sentries changing watch.
- **Olfactory**: Woodsmoke always. Wet wool and leather. Dog. The sour-sweet smell of the mead barrels. Tallow candles (beeswax is for the church). In summer, the reek of too many bodies; in winter, damp stone and mildew.
- **Tactile**: Cold. Nearly always cold. The stone walls radiate chill even in summer. The hearth provides warmth in a radius of ten feet; beyond that, you need your cloak. Drafts creep along the floor. In winter, your breath mists indoors.

**Key Locations Within the Fortress**:

1. **The Council Chamber** (where Chapter 1 opens)
   - Smaller than the Great Hall, more defensible
   - Roman frescoes still visible, faded: scenes of victory, order, imperial glory—now ironic
   - A round table (Arthur's innovation, not yet famous)
   - Braziers for heat, inadequate
   - Narrow windows looking out to the courtyard

2. **Arthur's Chamber**
   - Spartan by choice; Arthur distrusts luxury
   - A narrow bed, a writing desk (rare—most lords don't read)
   - Shelves with salvaged Roman texts: fragments of Aristotle, agricultural manuals, military treatises
   - No decoration except a single map (inaccurate) pinned to the wall

3. **The Chapel**
   - Repurposed Roman room; Christian imagery layered over pagan
   - Brother Marcus's domain
   - Smells of incense and old incense-smell absorbed into stone
   - Acoustic qualities make whispers audible—not good for secrets

4. **The Barracks**
   - Where the knights sleep (Arthur sleeps separately only because he must)
   - Rough camaraderie, dice games, weapon maintenance
   - The young knights idolize Arthur; the older ones are skeptical

5. **The Stables**
   - Horses are wealth; better maintained than human quarters
   - Arthur visits here to think—horses don't argue
   - Smell of hay, manure, leather, horse sweat

6. **The Kitchens**
   - Smoky, chaotic, presided over by a formidable woman (unnamed)
   - Food: bread, pottage, occasional meat, ale/mead
   - Arthur eats with his men when he can (symbolism matters)

**The Surrounding Landscape**:
- Rolling hills, forested (more trees then than now)
- Crumbling Roman road leading east (toward Saxon territory)
- Villages within a day's ride: timber-and-thatch, subsistence farming
- Old Roman villas, now abandoned or occupied by squatters
- A nearby river (transportation, water, winter floods)

### Secondary Setting: The Barrow

**Where the Mirror Is Kept**: Merlin hides the Mirror in an ancient barrow—a pre-Roman burial mound, older than anyone remembers.

**Approach**:
- A mile from Arthur's fortress, but feels farther
- Overgrown with brambles and thorn bushes (deliberate neglect)
- Local people avoid it ("bad luck," "cursed," "the old ones sleep there")
- The entrance: a low stone doorway, half-buried

**Interior**:
- Damp, smelling of earth and stone and time
- Low ceiling, oppressive
- The passage narrows, turns, descends
- Chamber at the heart: circular, beehive-domed, lined with massive stones
- The Mirror leans against the far wall, covered with a cloth
- No light except what you bring; torches cast huge shadows
- Silence is profound, almost physical
- The air tastes old

**Atmosphere**:
- The weight of time made tangible
- A sense of waiting, of something paused
- Cold that isn't just temperature
- Merlin's statement: "The barrow had waited a thousand years for this conversation"

### Tertiary Settings

**Villages in Arthur's Territory**:
- Timber-and-thatch construction; central well or stream
- Common fields surrounding (open field system)
- Headman's house (largest, still small)
- Smoke from cooking fires, animals in the streets
- Seasonal rhythms: plowing, planting, harvest, hungry winter
- The plague hits here first and hardest

**The Borderlands (Saxon Territory)**:
- Contested ground, burned fields, abandoned homesteads
- Saxon settlements pushing westward: different architecture, different gods
- The frontier is not a line but a zone of chaos

**Ruined Roman City** (visited briefly):
- Echoing streets, collapsed roofs, looted mosaics
- A few squatters in the ruins
- The forum overgrown with weeds
- Aqueduct broken, baths dry
- A visual representation of what Arthur is trying to prevent

**The Eastern Ports** (mentioned, not visited):
- Where the plague arrives from the Continent
- Trade continues despite collapse (diminished)
- Saxon ships, merchant vessels, rumors from across the sea

## 4. The Social World

### Political Structure: Arthur's Tenuous Authority

**The Sword in the Stone**:
- Arthur pulled the sword six months before the story opens
- This was accepted as divine sign by some, rejected by others
- Even those who accept the miracle resent a peasant king
- Arthur's legitimacy rests on: (1) the miracle, (2) his competence, (3) lack of better alternatives

**Who Supports Arthur**:
- **The Desperate**: Those who need strong leadership to survive Saxon raids
- **The Pragmatists**: Those who judge by results, not bloodline
- **The Faithful**: Christians who believe God chose Arthur
- **The Young**: Knights who see opportunity for merit-based advancement

**Who Opposes Arthur**:
- **Old Nobility**: Lords who believe rank should pass through blood
- **Regional Strongmen**: Warlords who want autonomy, not central authority
- **Saxon Collaborators**: Those who think accommodation is wiser than resistance
- **The Ambitious**: Rivals who want the throne for themselves

**The Round Table** (Arthur's Innovation):
- A council where voices are heard by merit, not rank
- Sitting in a circle denies precedence—radical symbolism
- Decisions made by evidence and argument
- Currently small: Arthur + 5-8 key supporters (will grow)
- This is where the systematization-of-magic proposal emerges (Part 2 crisis)

**Political Fragmentation**:
- Britain is not a unified kingdom but a patchwork of territories
- Tribal loyalties predate Roman occupation, survived it, resurge now
- Arthur is trying to build something that transcends tribe—a polity based on law
- This is why his failure (in the long term) is poignant: he's fighting entropy

**The Lords' Hierarchy** (traditional):
1. High King (contested; Arthur claims this)
2. Regional Kings (some recognize Arthur, some don't)
3. Lords/War Leaders (military power bases)
4. Landholders (small-scale nobility)
5. Freeman farmers (legally free but economically bound)
6. Serfs/Slaves (Rome's legacy; Arthur wants to change this)

### Religious Context: Christianity in Transition

**The Church's Position**:
- Christianity was Rome's official religion; it came with the Empire
- Now Rome is gone, but the Church remains (strongest institution)
- Two traditions in tension:
  - **Roman Christianity**: Hierarchical, orthodox, claims universal authority
  - **Celtic Christianity**: Decentralized, mystical, syncretic with older beliefs

**Brother Marcus** (Arthur's chaplain):
- Represents the Church in Arthur's court
- Educated (can read Latin), morally serious
- Genuinely believes magic is dangerous (devil's work? Or divine power misused?)
- Torn between loyalty to Arthur and obedience to Church doctrine
- His arc: from suspicion of Merlin → witnessing Merlin's restraint → uncertain acceptance

**The Pelagian Controversy**:
- Pelagius (British monk) taught that humans have free will, can choose good without grace
- Rome condemned this as heresy (original sin, need for grace)
- But in Britain, Pelagian ideas persist
- **Thematic resonance**: The Benefactor chose to democratize magic (free will applied to power); the catastrophe suggests some choices are too dangerous for mortals

**Religion and Magic**:
- The Church's official position: magic is devil's work
- Unofficial position: complicated. Miracles vs. sorcery—what's the difference?
- In practice: Church tolerates "white magic" (healing, blessings) and persecutes "black magic" (curses, divination)
- Hedge-witches are vulnerable; Christian villagers will turn on them if the harvest fails or a child dies

**Religious Practice** (daily life):
- Sunday Mass (attendance expected but not universal)
- Saints' days, liturgical calendar
- Prayer before meals, before battle, in illness
- Confession (Marcus hears Arthur's; this creates dramatic tension—what can Arthur confess?)
- Christianity is largely a religion of the higher-status; peasants blend it with older practices

**Pending Religious Persecution** (future conflict):
- Church faction demands action against magic-users
- Hedge-witches and cunning-folk rounded up, executed
- Forces Arthur to choose: protect magic users or appease the Church?
- Merlin must stay hidden while those like him burn
- This accelerates the separation of magical and non-magical communities

### Daily Life: Survival in Post-Roman Britain

**The Economy** (such as it is):
- Subsistence agriculture: most people farm, grow what they eat
- Barter economy in villages; coins rare (Roman coins still circulate, but minting has stopped)
- Arthur's court operates on tributary system: lords owe service, grain, soldiers
- Trade exists but is fragile: roads degrading, banditry common, Saxon raids disrupt
- Specialized crafts: smiths, potters, weavers, tanners (essential, relatively prosperous)

**Food**:
- Bread (barley, rye; wheat is luxury)
- Pottage (grain/vegetable stew, daily staple)
- Cheese, eggs (protein sources)
- Meat occasionally (pork, chicken; mutton; beef is rare)
- Fish in coastal/river areas
- Ale (water often unsafe)
- Honey (only sweetener; sugar unknown)
- Seasonal scarcity: spring is "hungry time" before harvest

**Clothing**:
- Wool and linen (the only fabrics)
- Tunics, cloaks, leg wrappings, leather shoes/boots
- Colors: undyed (off-white, brown, gray) for commoners; dyed for wealthy (vegetable dyes, muted colors)
- Arthur dresses simply despite being king (symbolic choice)
- Merlin: nondescript traveler's clothes, patched and re-patched, ageless

**Health and Medicine**:
- Life expectancy ~30-35 years (if you survive childhood)
- High infant mortality, death in childbirth common
- Illness is a constant threat: infection, plague, parasites, malnutrition
- Roman medical knowledge is fading; some texts survive, few can read them
- Healers: midwives, herbalists, bone-setters, prayer
- The plague that strikes in Part 1 is terrifyingly normal for this period

**Literacy**:
- Extremely rare; maybe 5% of population can read at all
- Latin is the written language; spoken vernacular is evolving toward Old English/Welsh
- Arthur can read (rare for his birth class; his mother taught him)
- Merlin reads languages no one else recognizes
- Books are precious, hand-copied, fragile
- Most knowledge transmission is oral

**Travel**:
- On foot or horseback (only the wealthy have horses)
- Roman roads are fastest but degrading, dangerous
- A day's travel: 20-30 miles on foot, 40-50 on horseback
- Bandits, wild animals, Saxon raiding parties
- Few inns; travelers beg hospitality or sleep rough

**Justice**:
- Traditional: blood-price, feuds, trial by combat, oaths
- Arthur is trying to implement: consistent laws, evidence-based judgment, impartial arbitration
- Resistance: people distrust this new system, prefer traditional ways
- Crimes: theft, murder, oath-breaking, adultery, desertion
- Punishments: fines, exile, mutilation, execution (no prisons; can't afford to feed criminals)

**War**:
- Constant low-level threat: Saxon raids, border skirmishes, internal feuds
- Armies are small (hundreds, not thousands)
- Heavy cavalry (Arthur's strength; Roman legacy)
- Infantry (spearmen, shield wall)
- No professional army; lords bring their followers when called
- Battles are rare; raids and ambushes more common
- The goal is usually not conquest but: taking livestock, burning crops, political intimidation

**The Collapse of Roman Infrastructure**:
- Aqueducts breaking, water systems failing
- Roads crumbling (no central authority to maintain them)
- Urban centers depopulating (people return to subsistence farming)
- Literacy declining, technical knowledge being lost
- This is the context for Arthur's struggle: he's trying to preserve/rebuild complex systems with inadequate resources
- **Thematic parallel**: Rome falling = civilization collapsing; magic separating = knowledge being restricted. Both are about choosing what to save when you can't save everything.

## 5. World Rules & Logic: The Magic System

### Magic in This Era: Pre-Institutional

**No Hogwarts, No Ministry, No Community**:
- Formal magical institutions won't exist for another 500+ years
- Hogwarts is founded ~990 CE (this story: ~475 CE)
- Magic-users are isolated, secretive, survival-oriented
- No shared terminology, no standardized spells, no common curriculum

**Who Has Magic**:
- Genetic: inherited through bloodlines (DNA markers as Atlantean authentication)
- Rare: maybe 1 in 1,000 births? (Less visible than later eras because of hiding)
- Magical children in non-magical families: usually die young (accidental magic draws lethal attention) or are abandoned as "cursed"
- Magical lineages hoard knowledge, marry among themselves when possible
- **Muggle-born** equivalent: rare in this era because magical bloodlines are already fragmented; a magical child in a non-magical family is seen as witchcraft, demonic possession, or divine curse

**Types of Magic-Users**:

1. **Hedge-Witches**:
   - Village-level practitioners, mostly women
   - Simple charms: protecting hearth, easing childbirth, finding lost objects
   - Herbalism blended with magic (which is which?)
   - Passed mother to daughter, oral tradition
   - Vulnerable: if crops fail or someone dies, they're blamed
   - The plague kills them at higher rates (magic makes them more vulnerable? Or targeted?)

2. **Cunning-Folk**:
   - Traveling practitioners, usually men
   - Sell amulets, potions, blessings, curses-for-hire
   - Con artists mixed with genuine practitioners (hard to tell)
   - Avoid staying in one place (safety in mobility)
   - Some have real power; most are frauds

3. **Wizards** (rare):
   - Powerful, learned, long-lived
   - Each is unique (no standardized training)
   - Hoard knowledge (that's what keeps them valuable)
   - May take apprentices, but carefully, rarely
   - Merlin is the most powerful in Britain (perhaps Europe)
   - The Saxon wizard is another example (uses power openly; Merlin refuses to)

4. **Clerical Magic**:
   - Does prayer work? Are miracles magic?
   - Unclear in-world; the Church says miracles are divine, magic is demonic
   - Some priests may have latent magical ability expressed as "holy" powers
   - Brother Marcus never shows overt magic, but he blesses water, prays over the sick—is there power in it?

**How Magic Is Learned**:
- **No books** (Interdict of Merlin prevents powerful magic being written down)
- Master to apprentice, parent to child
- Trial and error (dangerous)
- Some inherit instinctive knowledge (genetic memory?)
- Dreams, visions (is the Source of Magic communicating?)
- Each practitioner develops their own techniques, terminology, understanding

**What Magic Can Do** (in this era):
- Healing (limited; can't cure everything; dangerous to do openly)
- Divination (unreliable, feared)
- Curses (rarely effective; mostly psychological)
- Blessings/protections (same)
- Elemental manipulation (fire, water—small scale)
- Illusion, concealment (Merlin uses these)
- Transfiguration (only the most skilled; Merlin can)
- Combat magic (rare, devastating, Merlin refuses)
- Longevity (Merlin's secret; the Philosopher's Stone)

**What Magic CANNOT Do** (crucial for plot):
- Raise the dead (death is final)
- Create food from nothing (Gamp's Law applies)
- Cure the plague easily (complex biological systems resist magic? Or Merlin's refusal is strategic?)
- Force someone's will permanently (love potions are temporary, imperfect)
- Time travel (no Time-Turners yet, or Merlin has one and won't use it)
- Predict the future reliably (prophecy is vague, maddening)
- Replace systematic governance (magic can win a battle, not build a kingdom)

### The Deep Cosmology: HPMOR Integration

**Atlantis: The Erased Future**:
- Not ancient past, but a Star Trek-level civilization humanity was building toward
- Integrated magic and science to incredible heights
- The Benefactor (Atlantean engineer) tried to democratize magic universally
- This triggered a cascade that destroyed Atlantis so completely it was retroactively erased from causality
- Only the Mirror survived (origin point of catastrophe, lodged outside normal time)
- No one in 475 CE knows about Atlantis; no legends, no ruins, no memory
- Only Merlin knows, because the Mirror showed him

**The Source of Magic**:
- Created by Atlanteans as a fundamental force (see HPMOR canon)
- Functions as reality-warping engine with DNA-based authentication
- Enforces modified physics (Aristotelian broomsticks, conservation laws, Time-Turner restrictions)
- Built-in security features (Interdict of Merlin)

**Magic as Living, Wounded Force** (story's unique addition):
- Magic is not just a system but something with preferences, tendencies, something like awareness
- The Atlantean catastrophe was magic *hurting itself*—an autoimmune cascade
- Since then, magic is cautious, wary, flinching
- It doesn't reach toward minds as eagerly as it once did
- The "fading" of magic across centuries is not depletion but wariness
- Magic wants to trust again but learned fear

**The Interdict of Merlin**:
- Scar tissue from the catastrophe, not a spell Merlin cast
- Powerful magic resists written transmission (information won't transfer)
- This happened because self-replicating spells during the cascade "burned" that pathway
- Merlin discovered this, named it, understood it
- He could theoretically make it permanent (bind the wound forever with the Stone)
- He chooses not to—the wound is still healing, magic is still recovering
- **His daily choice**: Let it heal (risk another cascade) or bind it permanently (cripple magic forever)?

**The Mirror of Erised**:
- Atlantean artifact, survived the erasure because it was the epicenter
- Shows Coherent Extrapolated Volition (CEV): your wiser self's deepest values
- The Benefactor built it to "make the universe care"—a pocket of reality enforcing morality
- It worked, briefly, before the cascade
- Merlin found it centuries ago (in the barrow? Left by someone who fled the cascade?)
- It showed him the truth: Atlantis, the Benefactor, the cascade, the erasure
- When Arthur and Merlin stand before it together, it shows their combined CEV: both men before the wound, choosing to bind it for humanity's sake

**The Philosopher's Stone** (subtle Easter egg):
- Merlin possesses it (in HPMOR, Baba Yaga had it, but it's "a very old thing indeed")
- Grants immortality (why Merlin has lived centuries)
- Power is "imposition of permanency"
- This is the tool Merlin could use to make the Interdict permanent
- He holds the knife but doesn't use it
- Mentioned obliquely 2-3 times, never named explicitly
- To HPMOR readers: recognizable; to general readers: mysterious artifact

### Magical Artifacts in Arthur's Era

**Excalibur: The Sword of Borrowed Certainty**

The sword Arthur pulled from the stone is not merely a symbol of divine right—it is a magical artifact that grants its wielder strategic and tactical certainties. Knowledge without source. Arthur *knows* things he has no reason to know.

*How It Works*:
- Provides intuitive knowledge about threats, opportunities, and outcomes
- Arthur knows the Saxons will invade within two years—he simply *knows*
- The certainty feels borrowed, foreign, slightly manipulative
- Cannot distinguish between his own conclusions and the sword's influence
- Works only when worn/carried; the knowledge fades when separated from it

*Why It's Dangerous*:
- Arthur cannot trust his own thinking while wielding it
- He suspects Merlin is using the sword to control him, make him a puppet
- The sword's guidance may be correct but still represents manipulation
- Arthur's autonomy feels compromised—is he ruling or being ruled?

*Why Merlin Created/Gave It*:
- A prophecy told Merlin to give this artifact to whoever drew the sword from stone
- He doesn't know WHY prophecy demanded it
- He resents being prophecy-bound (echoes HPMOR: "Merlin wished to free us from prophecy")
- This resentment eventually drives Merlin to create the Hall of Prophecy—an attempt to understand, contain, and control prophetic magic
- He didn't realize the sword would teach Arthur to trust him

*Arthur's Arc With the Sword*:
- Initially distrusts it completely; views himself as Merlin's pawn
- Tries to rule without it, fails to anticipate obvious threats
- Realizes the certainties were GOOD—they helped him rule well
- The sword informed him; the choices were still his own
- This becomes a model for accepting guidance without full explanation
- The sword relationship prefigures his eventual acceptance of Merlin's larger secret

*Prophecy's Irony*:
- Merlin gave the sword because prophecy demanded it
- He didn't realize it would be what teaches Arthur to trust him
- Prophecy worked through Merlin even as he resented it
- Parallels Dumbledore's prophecy-bound manipulation of Harry in HPMOR

**Merlin's Hat: The Memory Repository** (Future Sorting Hat)

Merlin possesses a battered, ancient hat that serves as an external memory storage device. Too much knowledge for one mind to hold—even an immortal one. The hat holds what Merlin cannot carry alone.

*How It Works*:
- Must be WORN to access (direct mind-to-mind contact required)
- Two-way transfer: stores wearer's memories, shares stored memories with wearer
- Hat reads the wearer's question or need, provides relevant memories
- Cannot be queried from a distance or through writing
- The intimacy of wearing creates vulnerability—you can't casually access it

*What It Contains*:
- Centuries of Merlin's accumulated lore and observations
- Spells, historical knowledge, languages, names of the dead
- Memories of civilizations that no longer exist
- Knowledge too dangerous to write down but too important to forget
- Fragments of Atlantean understanding, filtered through Merlin's comprehension

*The Developing Personality*:
- Centuries of absorbed thoughts have created accretion of opinion
- The hat develops wit, preferences, judgment
- Not truly sentient, but something like personality emerges from accumulated memories
- It holds pieces of everyone who ever wore it

*Connection to the Interdict*:
- The hat is a loophole in the Interdict of Merlin
- Powerful magic can't be transmitted in text—but CAN be transmitted through direct mind-to-mind memory transfer
- Merlin exploits this carefully to preserve pre-Interdict knowledge
- One of the few repositories of truly ancient magical understanding

*Evolution to Sorting Hat*:
- Hogwarts founders (with Merlin's guidance?) eventually repurpose it
- Already full of Merlin's memories about what makes wizards wise, brave, cunning, loyal
- The hat uses that accumulated wisdom to evaluate students
- The personality is the accretion of centuries of absorbed thoughts—explains its wit and opinions
- The requirement to be worn persists: Sorting requires direct contact

*Narrative Function*:
- Explains how Merlin preserves knowledge across centuries without writing it down
- Creates interesting scenes where characters must physically wear the hat
- Seeds the origin of a beloved HPMOR/HP artifact
- Shows Merlin's relationship with memory, knowledge, and loneliness

**The Benefactor** (Atlantean engineer):
- Never named in the story (remains anonymous, universal)
- Genuinely good person, morally right to want democratization
- Succeeded in making magic accessible to all
- This destroyed everything
- Their face haunts the Mirror's memories
- Thematic core: *They were right and still ended the world*

### HPMOR Canon Compatibility Check

**What HPMOR Establishes**:
- The Interdict exists, prevents written transmission of powerful magic [Ch. 23]
- Atlantis destroyed itself [Ch. 25, 77]
- The Mirror has unusual properties tied to desire/CEV [Ch. 77, 110-111]
- Magic appears to be an engineered system [Ch. 108]
- The Philosopher's Stone exists, very ancient [Ch. 108]
- Merlin is historical (Interdict named after him) [Ch. 23]

**What This Story Adds** (compatible, not contradictory):
- Atlantis as erased future (HPMOR is vague on purpose; this fills the gap without contradicting)
- Magic as living/wounded force (inference from established facts, not contradiction)
- Interdict as scar tissue vs. Merlin's spell (reinterpretation of origin, compatible with effects)
- The Benefactor's story (new, but explains why Atlantis fell through *good* intentions)
- Merlin's choice to maintain vs. bind the Interdict (explains why it persists)
- Origin of wizard-Muggle separation (seeds planted in Arthur's era, formalized centuries later)

**No canon violations**: Everything in this world bible is compatible with HPMOR's established facts. The additions are in the deliberately unexplained spaces Eliezer left open.

## 6. Historical Context: Before, During, After

### Before the Story Begins

**Ancient Past** (pre-Roman):
- Celtic tribes occupy Britain, oral culture, Druids
- Iron Age hill forts, tribal warfare
- The barrow where Merlin hides the Mirror: built in this era (Bronze Age?)
- Old gods: nature spirits, ancestors, magic woven into the land

**Roman Occupation** (43-410 CE):
- Four centuries of Roman rule
- Infrastructure: roads, cities, aqueducts, baths, law, literacy
- Christianity arrives with the Empire (official religion by 380 CE)
- Latin becomes language of administration and Church
- Magical practitioners hide (Rome persecutes "witchcraft")

**The Withdrawal** (410 CE):
- Rome abandons Britain to defend the collapsing Empire
- Legions leave; Britain is told to "look to its own defenses"
- ~65 years before this story opens
- Within a generation, systems collapse: no central government, no maintained infrastructure, trade diminishes, cities empty, warlords rise

**The Sword in the Stone** (6 months before story):
- Old king dies with no clear heir (disputed succession)
- Magical test: sword embedded in stone/anvil, "rightful king" can pull it
- Who set this test? (Merlin? The Source of Magic? Unclear—Arthur doesn't know)
- Arthur (14, peasant-born, educated by his mother) pulls the sword
- Some accept the divine sign; others reject a peasant king
- Arthur has been king for six months: enough time to start reforms, not enough to secure power

**Merlin's Arrival** (uncertain timing):
- Merlin has been at court for... weeks? Months? Years?
- Arthur doesn't know Merlin's true age or origin
- Merlin presented himself as wandering scholar/healer
- Useful enough that Arthur keeps him despite distrust

### During the Story (~475-477 CE)

**Year One: The Plague and Investigations**
- Spring 475: Story opens, plague arrives from eastern ports
- Summer 475: Plague peaks, Merlin heals selectively, Arthur investigates

## 7. Atmosphere Guide: Sensory Palette & Tone

- **Fragility**: Everything is breaking—Rome's roads, Arthur's legitimacy, trust, hope, the body during plague
- **Stubborn Endurance**: But people endure; they rebuild, they hope, they try again
- **Loneliness**: Arthur is isolated by his crown; Merlin by his knowledge; both by choices no one else can understand

**Moments of Warmth** (essential for balance):
- The hearth fire in the Great Hall (physical warmth, community, shared meals)
- Camaraderie among the knights (rough affection, loyalty)
- Arthur's friendship with Lancelot (peer relationship, mutual respect)

**Wry Humor** (to cut the philosophy):
- Arthur's internal monologue can be wry, self-aware
- Merlin occasionally delivers bone-dry observations

## 8. Thematic Resonance: How Setting Reflects Theme

### Core Theme: The Burden of Knowledge

**How the World Embodies This**:

1. **Rome's Lost Knowledge** = **Atlantis's Lost Civilization**
   - Arthur lives in a world where his ancestors knew how to build aqueducts, straight roads, underfloor heating, and he can't replicate it
   - Merlin lives knowing a civilization built wonders and destroyed itself
   - Both face the question: what do you do with knowledge you can't safely share?
   - Arthur sees books he can't read (literacy lost); Merlin sees truths he can't speak (Interdict, and beyond it, his choice)

2. **The Collapse of Systems** = **The Danger of Integration**
   - Rome fell because complex interdependent systems broke
   - Atlantis fell because complex integrated magic cascaded
   - Arthur tries to build systems; Merlin prevents magical systematization
   - Both are trying to prevent total collapse, in different ways

3. **The Post-Roman Dark Age** = **The Post-Atlantean Caution of Magic**
   - After Rome: Britain fragments, knowledge is lost, people survive in smaller, simpler communities
   - After Atlantis: Magic pulls away, becomes cautious, accessible only in limited ways
   - Both are recoveries from catastrophe
   - Both are dark ages that could last centuries (magic's still hasn't fully ended)

4. **Arthur's Isolation as King** = **Merlin's Isolation as Guardian**
   - Arthur can't explain some decisions (based on trust in Merlin, not evidence)
   - Merlin can't explain his refusals (based on knowledge he can't share)
   - Both are lonely at the top/depth of their respective burdens
   - The story is about that loneliness ending through mutual recognition

### Secondary Themes Reflected in Setting

**Trust Without Understanding**:
- Arthur must trust Merlin about things he can't verify (magic, Atlantis, the danger)
- Peasants must trust Arthur about systematic reforms they don't understand (quarantine, evidence-based law)
- Soldiers must trust their commander's strategy when they can't see the whole battlefield
- This is the epistemological problem of authority: when must you trust expertise beyond your ability to evaluate?

**Choosing What to Save When You Can't Save Everything**:
- Arthur can't save every village, stop every Saxon raid, heal every patient, reform every injustice
- He must choose: which roads to maintain, which alliances to prioritize, which traditions to discard
- Merlin can't save every magical practitioner, preserve every spell, prevent every misuse
- He must choose: which knowledge to pass on, which to let die
- The triage is constant, exhausting, heartbreaking

**The Tension Between Justice and Survival**:
- Justice demands democratizing magic (the Benefactor was RIGHT)
- Survival demands restricting it (the Benefactor destroyed everything)
- Justice demands overthrowing the old nobility (they're corrupt, incompetent)
- Survival demands working with them (they control resources, armies)
- Arthur lives this tension daily; Merlin has lived it for centuries

**"No Rescuer Hath the Rescuer"**:
- Arthur has no one wiser to turn to (Merlin seems wiser but won't explain)
- Merlin has had no one to share the burden for centuries
- This is Godric Gryffindor's insight from HPMOR: the one who saves everyone has no one to save them
- The story's resolution: they save each other, not through action but through witness

## 10. World-Building Economy: Detail Levels

### Essential Details (Must Be Consistent)

**These elements appear repeatedly and must remain constant**:

1. **Arthur's Age**: 14 going on 15 (young, prodigy, idealistic, forgivable mistakes)
2. **Time Since Sword**: 6 months (established authority, not fully secure)
3. **Rome's Withdrawal**: ~65 years ago (living memory of grandparents, systemic collapse ongoing)
4. **Christianity's Presence**: Official but contested; Celtic vs. Roman strains; Church suspicious of magic
5. **Literacy**: Extremely rare; Arthur reads (unusual); Merlin reads languages no one recognizes
6. **Magic Visibility**: Very low; no institutions, no community, isolated practitioners
7. **The Plague's Impact**: Kills magic-users at higher rates (why? Unexplained but consistent)
8. **The Interdict**: Prevents written transmission of powerful magic; this is known (vaguely) even to non-magical people as "old knowledge can't be recovered from books"
9. **Merlin's Presentation**: Wandering scholar, indeterminate age, frustratingly opaque, useful enough to keep around
10. **The Mirror's Location**: Hidden in the barrow, a mile from Arthur's fortress, avoided by locals

### Supporting Details (Should Be Consistent If Mentioned)

**These add texture but don't drive plot; consistency matters but they're not central**:

1. **Food**: Bread, pottage, occasional meat, ale; hunger in spring before harvest
2. **Clothing**: Wool, linen, undyed for commoners, Arthur dresses simply
3. **Weather**: Cold, wet, misty; Britain's climate
4. **Horses**: Valuable, military asset, Arthur's tactical advantage
5. **The Round Table**: Innovation in Arthur's court, 5-8 members initially
6. **Brother Marcus**: Chaplain, educated, conflicted about Merlin
7. **Bedivere**: Military advisor, appears in Ch. 1, loyal
8. **Saxon Language/Culture**: Different from Romano-British, worships different gods
9. **Coinage**: Roman coins still circulate, but no new minting; mostly barter economy
10. **Seasonal Cycle**: Agricultural rhythms, planting/harvest, hungry winter

### Background Details (Flavor Only)

**These can be mentioned for atmosphere but don't need rigorous consistency**:

1. **Specific village names**: Can be invented as needed
2. **Minor nobles' names/territories**: Background color
3. **Types of trees**: Oak, ash, thorn—whatever fits the moment
4. **Bird calls, animal sounds**: Atmospheric detail
5. **Specific foods at a meal**: Vary for realism
6. **Weather on a given day**: Unless plot-relevant, can shift for mood
7. **Minor servants' names**: Background characters
8. **Specific prayers/blessings**: Christian flavor, don't need to be historically precise
9. **Trade goods**: Mentioned in passing, don't need detailed economics
10. **Distant kingdoms**: Can be vaguely referenced without detail

### Deliberately Vague (Narrative Freedom)

**These are intentionally unspecified to allow flexibility**:

1. **Exact Location of Arthur's Fortress**: "Western Britain" is sufficient; don't pin to a modern map
2. **Precise Year**: "~475 CE" is enough; specific dates aren't needed
3. **Arthur's Parentage**: "Peasant-born, mother educated him" is the key fact; don't need detailed genealogy
4. **How Merlin Found the Mirror**: Can be revealed (or not) as story needs
5. **Other Wizards in Britain**: Are there any? How many? Leave ambiguous unless plot needs them
6. **The Philosopher's Stone's History**: "A very old thing indeed"; deliberately mysterious
7. **What Happened to Atlantis (Exactly)**: The Mirror shows fragments, not a documentary; leave impressionistic
8. **The Benefactor's Name**: Never revealed; they remain universal, archetypal
9. **Merlin's True Age**: "Centuries" is sufficient; exact number doesn't matter
10. **Future History**: What happens after Arthur dies? Vague is better; the legend grows, details are lost

## 12. Writer's Practical Guide

### When Writing a Scene, Ask:

1. **Where are we?** (Choose from location table)
2. **What does it smell like?** (Smoke, stone, earth, bodies, food)
3. **What's the temperature?** (Default: cold; warmth is notable)
4. **What sounds are present?** (Wind, echoes, horses, voices)
5. **What's the light source?** (Hearth, torches, candles, daylight through narrow windows)
6. **What's the season?** (Affects weather, food, mood, illness)
7. **Who else is present?** (Servants, guards, knights—the world is populated)
8. **What's the physical state of things?** (Crumbling, patched, maintained, failing)

### Atmosphere Checklist:

- [ ] At least one sensory detail per scene (sight, sound, smell, temperature, texture)
- [ ] Physical discomfort mentioned if present (cold, hunger, exhaustion, illness)
- [ ] The weight of something (crown, knowledge, sword, responsibility)
- [ ] A moment of warmth or light (to balance the darkness)
- [ ] A reminder that systems are fragile (something breaking, failing, needing repair)

### Voice Consistency:

**Arthur** (14 years old, prodigy, idealistic):
- Thinks in systems and patterns
- Observes details others miss
- Internal monologue: questioning, analyzing, occasionally wry
- Dialogue: clear, direct, occasionally too blunt for politics
- Mistakes: impulsive, assumes people are rational, trusts systems over individuals
- Growth: learns when to trust what he can't verify

**Merlin** (centuries old, burdened, careful):
- Speaks sparingly; every word is chosen
- Dialogue: layered, can be read multiple ways
- Internal monologue: weighted with history, loneliness, endless calculation
- Silences are as important as speech
- Shows emotion through small gestures, not grand displays
- Growth: learns to share the weight, tentatively, with Arthur

### Historical Authenticity Notes:

**Avoid**:
- Modern idioms ("okay," "cool," "guys")
- Anachronistic technology (stirrups weren't common yet, no crossbows in Britain, no plate armor)
- Clean, well-lit environments (everything is darker, smokier, dirtier than modern)
- Easy travel (it's slow, dangerous, exhausting)
- Reliable information (news travels slowly, gets distorted)

**Include**:
- Physical hardship (cold, hunger, illness, exhaustion)
- Superstition blended with observation
- Oral culture (stories, songs, memory, not books)
- Seasonal rhythms (life follows agricultural calendar)
- Violence as constant background threat

### Magic Scene Guidelines:

**When showing magic**:
- Keep it rare (every instance should feel significant)
- Make it cost something (effort, risk, consequence)
- Don't explain mechanics (Merlin doesn't monologue about how spells work)
- Show Merlin's reluctance (he's hiding, not showing off)
- Demonstrate limitation (what magic can't do matters more than what it can)

**The Mirror scene specifically**:
- Build slowly (don't rush the revelation)
- Use sensory details (the barrow, the darkness, the Mirror's strange light)
- Layer the visions (Arthur sees Atlantis, the Benefactor, the cascade, in stages)
- Let silence do work (pauses between revelations)
- End on emotional truth, not explanation (Arthur's acceptance, not Merlin's lecture)

## Conclusion: The World as Character

This world—post-Roman Britain with its collapsing systems, hidden magic, and terrible secrets—is not just a backdrop. It is a character in the story, shaped by the same forces that shape Arthur and Merlin: the weight of knowledge, the fragility of civilization, the necessity of choosing what to preserve when you cannot save everything.

The cold stone halls are Arthur's burden made tangible. The barrow's darkness is Merlin's loneliness given form. The crumbling Roman roads are the Interdict visualized: knowledge that once connected everything, now fragmenting into isolated pieces.

When Arthur stands in his council chamber beneath faded frescoes of Roman glory, he is living the question: Can I rebuild what was lost? Should I?

When Merlin stands in the barrow before the Mirror, he is living the answer: Some things must stay buried. Some knowledge must be restricted. Not from tyranny, but from love.

The world of *The Weight of Atlantis* is one where every choice has cost, every system is fragile, every truth is complicated. It is a world that requires its heroes to accept limitation, embrace uncertainty, and bear weight without recognition or rest.

It is, in other words, our world—with the volume turned up.
