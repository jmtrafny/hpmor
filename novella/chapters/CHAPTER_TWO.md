# The Weight of Atlantis

## Chapter Two: The Weight of Centuries

---

The boy reminded him of someone. Someone whose face had been erased along with their entire civilization.

Merlin stood in his chambers, watching the candle flame. It flickered in the draft from the shuttered window—ancient oak, poorly fitted, the gaps wide enough to let winter through. He should repair it. He never did. Five hundred years, and he had never bothered to make himself comfortable.

Comfort was a trap. A suggestion that he might stay in one place long enough to care about such things.

The flame danced. Merlin watched it and did not age. He had watched flames for longer than this kingdom had existed. Had watched empires rise and fall, languages evolve and die, forests grow where cities once stood. The fire did not touch him. Nothing did. Not time, not blade, not the weight of memory.

*The Stone's gift*, he thought distantly. *And its curse.*

He touched his chest, where something hard and cold rested beneath the layers of rough wool. A very old thing indeed. Old enough to remember when the world had been different. When magic had flowed like water, generous and trusting, before—

Before.

Merlin closed his eyes and saw the council meeting again. Saw Arthur—fourteen years old, gangly and sharp-eyed—making the terrible choice about the plague refugees. Allow the border closures. Provide supplies. A cold calculus of lives saved and lives abandoned.

The boy had looked sick when he gave the order. Had understood exactly what he was doing, and done it anyway.

*Exactly*, Merlin thought, *how the Benefactor would have approached it.*

The similarity made his hands shake.

He remembered the first time he had seen the Benefactor's face—not in flesh, for they had never met, but in the Mirror. Centuries ago, when Merlin was younger and the wound was fresh and the world still remembered, in its dreams, what it had lost.

The Benefactor had been kind. That was the terrible thing. Not cruel, not power-mad, not driven by greed or ambition. Just *kind*. A systematic mind paired with genuine compassion, looking at a world of suffering and thinking: *I can fix this.*

They had approached the problem like Arthur approached the plague. Identified the vector of suffering—magic hoarded by a privileged few. Designed a solution—universal access, democratized power, knowledge shared freely. Implemented it carefully, testing at each stage, documenting results.

It had worked.

God help them, it had *worked*.

Merlin opened his eyes. The candle flame blurred, doubled. He blinked the moisture away. Five centuries, and his eyes still wept when he remembered. As if tears could wash away the past. As if grief could resurrect the dead.

*Is he the same?* The question circled in his mind like a crow over carrion. *Will he make the same choice?*

He saw Arthur's face overlaid with the Benefactor's—both young, both brilliant, both certain that reason could redeem the world. Both looking at suffering and refusing to accept it as inevitable. Both dangerous precisely because they were *right*.

The most catastrophic failures began with being right.

Merlin moved to the window. Through the gaps in the shutters, he could see the courtyard below. Torches burned along the walls—waste of resources, Arthur had noted, they should calculate optimal spacing for coverage. The boy thought in systems. Everything a mechanism to be understood, optimized, improved.

It was beautiful and terrible to watch. Like seeing a blade forged that might cut either way.

In the courtyard, a figure moved through sword forms. Even at this distance, even in firelight, Merlin recognized the angular grace. Arthur, practicing despite exhaustion. Drilling the same overhead cut again and again, trying to build muscle memory that would overcome his peasant's clumsiness with noble weapons.

The boy was trying. God and all the forgotten powers, he was *trying*. Trying to be worthy of a crown he hadn't wanted. Trying to save a kingdom that was already falling apart. Trying to make rational choices in a world that refused to be rational.

*And I am testing him*, Merlin thought. The knowledge tasted like ash. *Watching him struggle. Refusing to help. Waiting to see if he proves wise enough—or dangerous enough—to matter.*

When had he become this? When had five centuries of watching civilizations die made him into something that could look at a fourteen-year-old boy carrying an impossible weight and think: *Good. Let him break. Let him fail now, before he can destroy everything.*

The sword form stuttered. Arthur stopped, breathing hard, and Merlin saw him touch the blade's hilt—that unconscious gesture the boy made when he was trying to remember something he couldn't quite grasp.

*He knows something happened*, Merlin thought. *When he drew the sword. He felt it, even if he doesn't remember.*

The sword had been Merlin's test. A simple binding—nothing dramatic, nothing that would show on the surface. Just a small piece of magic threaded through steel and stone, designed to recognize a particular quality of mind. Not destiny. Not bloodline. Just the ability to see a problem and care more about solving it than about being right.

Arthur had drawn the sword because he was the kind of person who would study a plague while people died, not despite their suffering but *because* of it. Because saving the next village mattered more than his own comfort with helplessness.

The binding had shown Arthur a fragment of something—what the world needed him to be, perhaps. What he *could* be, if he grew wise instead of merely clever. Merlin didn't know exactly what the boy had seen. The binding showed different truths to different minds.

But he knew Arthur had forgotten it. The human mind was not meant to hold such weight. It shied away, buried the memory like flesh closing over a wound.

Which meant Arthur would have to *choose* again. Not because a magical binding told him to, but because he understood. Because he had grown wise enough to bear what the binding had tried to show him.

*If* he grew wise enough.

Merlin turned from the window. His chambers were sparse—a bed he rarely used, a table scarred with age, the candle burning low. No tapestries, no comforts, nothing to suggest permanence. The chambers of a man who had never unpacked because he had never truly arrived.

He moved to the small chest in the corner. Opened it with hands that had opened it ten thousand times. Inside: traveling clothes, a few books written in languages that no longer existed, and beneath them—

His fingers brushed the Mirror's frame.

He did not take it out. Never took it out, except when he traveled. Too dangerous to look at it here, where he might be interrupted. Where someone might see what it showed.

But he could feel it. The wood was warm beneath his fingertips, thrumming with a vitality that nothing else in this dead world possessed. The Mirror was a survivor too. The only other thing that remembered Atlantis.

The only other thing that knew what Merlin carried.

He closed the chest. Returned to the candle. Let his mind drift to the choice he made every day.

The wound was still there. He could feel it in the world's fabric, like scar tissue pulling tight across old trauma. The Interdict, people called it. *Merlin's Interdict*, as if he had created it. As if he had been the one to wound magic so deeply it had flinched away from human touch.

He had not created it. He had only discovered it. Had looked into the Mirror and understood what had happened—what must *always* happen when magic trusted too freely. What would happen again if the scar tissue healed.

The Interdict was magic's defense mechanism. A flinch, frozen into permanence. *I was hurt*, magic said through its absence. *I will not be hurt again.*

Powerful knowledge could not be written down. Could not be taught systematically. Could not spread through books or schools or well-meaning democratization. The teachers forgot what they tried to pass on. The students failed to understand what their teachers had known. Magic itself *resisted* the attempt to make it reproducible.

Not because of Merlin's will. Because of its own wounded nature.

But the wound was healing.

Each generation, the scar tissue weakened slightly. Each century, magic became a little more teachable, a little more systematic, a little more willing to trust. In a thousand years—perhaps two thousand, perhaps only five hundred, he could not predict precisely—the wound would be fully healed.

Magic would be generous again. Would trust again. Would let itself be systematized and taught and spread universally.

And someone brilliant and kind would look at the world's suffering and think: *I can fix this.*

And they would be right.

And it would destroy everything.

Merlin touched the Stone beneath his robe. Felt its weight, familiar as his own heartbeat. He could end this uncertainty. Right now. Tonight.

The Stone was permanence made solid. It could take the Interdict—that scar tissue, that flinch—and make it *eternal*. Not just magic defending itself, but magic *bound* from ever healing. The wound frozen forever, magic crippled permanently, the generous trust that had once let civilizations rise now locked away where it could never hurt anyone again.

He had the power. Had carried it for five hundred years. Had stood before the Mirror and seen his own extrapolated volition—the version of himself who knew everything, who understood all consequences—calmly choosing to bind the wound forever.

And every day for five centuries, Merlin had chosen not to do it.

*Hope*, he thought bitterly. *The most dangerous substance in the universe.*

Hope that humanity would grow wise enough. That the next generation would see what the last had not. That someone would finally understand the terrible arithmetic of cascading trust, would learn to choose restriction not from fear but from love.

Hope that he was *wrong*. That there was another way. That the Mirror's certainty was not as final as it seemed.

Hope that he would not have to be the one to cripple magic forever.

And now, watching Arthur in the courtyard, Merlin felt that hope kindle again—fragile and terrible, like a flame in a room full of kindling.

The boy was brilliant. Idealistic. Everything the world needed. Everything that could destroy it.

Exactly what the Benefactor had been.

Arthur stopped his practice. Stood for a moment in the torchlight, breathing hard, then looked up—directly at Merlin's window.

He could not possibly see Merlin in the darkness. But he looked anyway, as if he knew he was being watched. As if he understood, on some level deeper than consciousness, that every choice he made was being weighed.

Their eyes met across the distance and the darkness.

Merlin saw the boy's face—exhausted, determined, carrying a weight no fourteen-year-old should have to bear. Saw the plague decision written in the tension of his shoulders. Saw the man he would become, if he survived. If he grew wise instead of merely clever. If he learned what the binding had tried to teach him.

*Will you make the same choice?* Merlin wondered. *Will you see the wound and try to heal it? Will your kindness destroy us all?*

He did not know. Could not know. The Mirror showed him his *own* coherent extrapolated volition—what he would want if he knew everything—but it could not show him what Arthur would become.

That was still undecided.

That was why Merlin was still here.

Arthur held his gaze for another moment, then turned away. Continued his practice. The sword flashed in the torchlight, and Merlin saw grace beginning to emerge from clumsiness. The boy was learning. Growing. Becoming something that might be worthy of the crown he wore.

Or something that might doom them all.

Merlin watched until the candle guttered low. Watched until Arthur finally stopped, sheathed the sword, walked back toward the keep with the heavy gait of exhaustion. Watched until the courtyard was empty except for shadows and the wind.

Then he spoke, very quietly, to the darkness and the flame:

"Five hundred years ago, I learned the most dangerous substance in the universe was neither fire nor poison nor magic."

The candle flickered. The wind moved through the gaps in the shutters.

"It was hope."

His hands were shaking again. Merlin looked down at them—old hands, lined and scarred, unchanged by time. The Stone's gift. He could stand here for another five hundred years, watching civilizations rise and fall, waiting for someone wise enough to share the burden.

Or he could bind the wound tonight. Make the choice his extrapolated self had already made. Accept that humanity would never be wise enough, that the generous trust magic once possessed was too dangerous to resurrect, that restriction was the only mercy left.

He had chosen hope for five centuries.

And watching Arthur in the courtyard—brilliant, idealistic, dangerous Arthur—he felt it kindle again despite everything. Felt the terrible, treacherous possibility that *this time* might be different. That this boy might understand. That Merlin might finally be able to explain what he carried, and why, and find someone to stand with him in the choosing.

*I have started hoping again*, Merlin realized.

The thought should have terrified him.

Instead, he felt something else. Something he had not felt in so long he barely recognized it.

Relief.

Five hundred years of alone. Five hundred years of watching and weighing and wondering if he had already become the monster his silence made him seem. Five hundred years of carrying the weight until his bones bent under it.

And now, this peasant boy with his systematic thinking and his terrible choices. This child who looked at suffering and refused to call it inevitable. This king who did not yet know what kingship would cost him.

*He might be ready*, Merlin thought. *Not now. Not yet. But someday.*

*Or he might be the one who finally breaks the world.*

Both were possible. Both were terrifying.

But at least—for the first time in centuries—Merlin was not deciding alone.

He touched the Stone one more time. Felt its patient weight, its offer of permanence. The power to end the uncertainty tonight. To bind the wound forever and accept the loneliness as the price of safety.

*Not yet*, he told it. Told himself. Told the ghost of the Benefactor, wherever they existed in the void where Atlantis had been. *Not yet.*

He would wait. Would watch. Would test Arthur until he was certain—one way or another—whether the boy would choose wisdom or catastrophe.

And if Arthur chose catastrophe, if he proved to be another Benefactor with their terrible kindness and their brilliant solutions...

Then Merlin would bind the wound before the cascade could begin.

Would cripple magic forever.

Would condemn humanity to permanent separation from the generous trust that had once made civilizations soar.

Would become, finally and irrevocably, the tyrant everyone already half-believed he was.

But he would do it. If he had to. If Arthur forced the choice.

*Please*, Merlin thought, to no one and nothing, to the boy sleeping somewhere in the keep below. *Please be wise. Please understand. Please do not make me choose between your brilliant kindness and the world's survival.*

*Please do not make me become what I have spent five centuries trying not to be.*

The candle went out.

Merlin stood in the darkness, old hands pressed against his chest where the Stone rested. Feeling its weight. Feeling the wound in the world's fabric, that scar tissue slowly, inevitably healing.

Feeling hope—dangerous, treacherous hope—that he might not have to bear this alone forever.

Outside, the wind moved through the courtyard. Arthur's footsteps had faded. The torches burned low. The night settled over the keep like a blanket over someone who could not wake.

Merlin did not move. Did not sleep. Did not do anything except stand and remember and choose, once again, to wait.

To hope.

To believe that this time, somehow, might be different.

*Five hundred years ago*, he had said, *I learned the most dangerous substance in the universe was hope.*

And he had started hoping again.

God and all the forgotten powers help them both.

*End of Chapter Two*
