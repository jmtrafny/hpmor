# The Weight of Knowing

## Chapter One: The Sword and the Stone

---

The throne was harder than the ground he used to sleep on.

Arthur shifted, trying to find a position that didn't make his back ache, and failed. Six months since the sword. Six months since the world had looked at a peasant boy and decided he was king. The stonemasons were already at work on a proper throne—cushioned, they promised, with velvet from the continent—but for now he sat on cold granite and listened to reports of the empire's collapse.

"Rome has fallen." Lord Ector's voice carried the particular weight of a man delivering news he did not fully believe. "The Visigoths sacked the city. The legions will not be returning."

Arthur kept his face still. He had heard the traders talking, back in the village—the ones who came through twice a year with salt and iron tools. They spoke of empty forts along Hadrian's Wall, of Roman roads slowly being reclaimed by forest, of letters sent to the continent that never received replies. His father—his foster father, he reminded himself, the man who had raised him—had served in a Roman auxiliary unit forty years ago. "The legions were already ghosts when I was young," he'd told Arthur once, deep in his cups. "We just hadn't admitted it yet."

But he did not say this. He had learned, in his years as a peasant, which thoughts to keep behind his teeth.

"And the plague?" Arthur asked instead.

"Spreading." This from Brother Marcus, who served as the closest thing the court had to a physician—though his training was more prayer than poultice. "The eastern ports are worst hit. We've closed the harbors at Londinium, but—" He spread his hands in a gesture of helplessness.

*Closing the harbors does nothing if the sickness has already come ashore*, Arthur thought. He remembered the plague when it had struck his village, two winters before the sword. How the healers and the priests and the undertakers—the ones who handled the sick and the dead—had worn masks stuffed with herbs and poultices, thick cloths bound over their mouths and noses. And how they had lived when others died.

He had watched. He had counted.

The village priest had visited every sickroom, praying over the afflicted. He wore the herb-mask when he did. He never caught the plague. But old Marta's son, who had brought the sickness home from the eastern markets—he had worn no mask when he kissed his mother's cheek. And Marta had died screaming.

Arthur had reasoned it out, sitting in the barn with a stick scratching figures in the dirt. *The mask-wearers live. The bare-faced die.* The conclusion seemed obvious: the plague traveled through the air, through breath and vapor. If everyone wore the herb-masks the healers wore, perhaps the spread would slow. Perhaps his village would survive.

He had taken this observation to the village priest.

The priest had beaten him for "questioning God's punishment."

*You cannot reason your way out of divine judgment, boy. The plague takes whom the Lord wills. Your masks and your counting are the devil's arithmetic.*

A month later, half the village was dead. Including the priest.

Arthur had not felt satisfaction. Only a cold, terrible clarity: being right meant nothing if no one would listen.

"Continue the harbor closures," Arthur said. The words tasted like ash—he suspected the closures were useless, the sickness already spreading inland through a hundred vectors he couldn't see. "And send observers to the eastern ports. Not healers—we have no cure, and pretending otherwise wastes lives. I want men who can watch and record. Which houses fall ill first. Whether it follows the roads or the rivers. Whether those who survive can catch it again."

Brother Marcus frowned. "You wish to... study the plague, Your Majesty? While people are dying?"

"People will die whether we study it or not. But if we learn how it moves, we might save the next village. And the one after that."

*And perhaps we will learn I was wrong about the masks*, Arthur thought. *Perhaps the healers lived for some other reason—the herbs themselves, or divine protection, or simple luck. That is what observation is for: to discover where your assumptions fail.*

The advisors exchanged glances. Arthur had seen those glances before—the silent communication of adults who believed children could not read faces. *He's strange*, their eyes said. *Unnatural. Perhaps the sword chose wrong.*

Let them think it. He had work to do.

"The refugees," Arthur said. "From the plague-struck ports. Where are they going?"

Lord Ector shifted uncomfortably. "Inland, Your Majesty. They flee to family in the western holdings, or they simply... wander. Looking for anywhere the sickness hasn't reached."

"And we're letting them travel freely?"

Ector hesitated. "There is no law preventing it, Your Majesty. Though some lords have begun closing their borders of their own accord."

*No law*, Arthur thought. *Because no one has imagined needing one.* The Romans had built roads for legions to march on, not for refugees to carry plague across the land. In a functioning empire, central authority would coordinate the response. In Britain, each lord made his own choice.

"The refugees are carrying the plague with them," Arthur said slowly, thinking it through as he spoke. "Every village they pass through, every family that takes them in—they spread it further. The more they travel, the more people die."

Silence. The advisors looked at one another.

"What would you have us do, Your Majesty?" Ector asked carefully. "Turn them back? Let them starve on the roads?"

"No. We establish way stations." Arthur rose from the throne—ignoring the ache in his legs from the cold granite—and moved to the map spread across the council table. "Here, here, and here." He marked positions with his finger. "Anyone fleeing the eastern ports must stop at a way station for a fortnight. If they show no signs of sickness after fourteen days, they may continue. If they sicken, they stay until they recover or..." He didn't finish the sentence.

"You wish to imprison healthy citizens," Ector said flatly. "Held against their will."

"I wish to *save* healthy citizens. The ones in the western villages who don't yet know the plague is coming for them." Arthur looked up from the map. "Lord Ector, if a man's house is on fire, do you let him run into a crowd carrying burning timbers? Or do you stop him at the door until the flames are out?"

"Men are not timbers, Your Majesty."

"No. They're worse. Timbers don't have families who hide them when the authorities come looking. Timbers don't lie about where they've been."

He caught movement in his peripheral vision. In the shadows by the eastern pillar, half-hidden behind a tapestry depicting some ancient king's victory, stood a figure he had learned to notice.

Merlin.

The old man had not moved throughout the entire discussion. Had not spoken. Had barely seemed to breathe. He simply watched, as he always watched, with those dark eyes that saw everything and offered nothing.

*Charlatan*, Arthur thought—not for the first time. He had known men like Merlin in the village. "Cunning folk" who sold charms against evil spirits and potions for fertility, who maintained their mystique through cryptic pronouncements and convenient excuses. *The spell didn't work because you didn't believe hard enough. The charm failed because you angered the spirits.* Always an explanation that blamed the victim.

But this was not the time to deal with Merlin. The plague would not wait for Arthur to unmask a fraud.

He turned back to the council. "The way stations will have food, shelter, and what healers we can spare. No one will starve. No one will be turned away. But no one leaves until we're certain they won't carry the plague to new victims."

"The lords of the western holdings will never accept this," Bedivere said quietly. "Their people will see it as tyranny. The crown, imprisoning the innocent."

"The crown, protecting the innocent. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Bedivere met his eyes. "To the man held against his will, does it matter why?"

Arthur held the older man's gaze. "Ask me that question again when the plague has passed. Ask the families whose villages are still standing."

---

The plague discussion wound down into logistics—how many way stations, how to staff them, where to find the grain to feed refugees who might be held for weeks. Arthur let Lord Ector take the lead on details. The man had managed supplies for the old king's campaigns; he knew the mathematics of feeding people who didn't want to be where they were.

"And the Saxons?" Arthur asked, when the plague conversation had exhausted itself.

Lord Bedivere stepped forward—a younger man, barely twenty, but already scarred from a dozen border skirmishes. "Raiding parties along the eastern coast. Small groups so far. Testing our defenses."

"They're not testing. They're scouting."

Bedivere's jaw tightened. "With respect, Your Majesty, I have fought these raiders for three years. They come for plunder—grain, cattle, women. They test our response times, probe for weak points in our coastal watches. It is the same pattern every spring."

"And every spring, have the raids grown larger or smaller?"

A pause. "Larger, Your Majesty. But that is simply—"

"More men. Better coordination. Longer expeditions." Arthur leaned forward.

*Think it through*, he told himself. *What is the simplest explanation that fits all the observations?*

"Lord Bedivere, when a fox circles a henhouse, does it strike immediately? Or does it learn the farmer's routines first? When does he sleep? When does he check the coop? Where are the gaps in the fence?"

"Saxons are not foxes, Your Majesty. They are warriors."

"Warriors led by chiefs who want to *win*. The Saxons know Rome has fallen—that news has reached every shore in the known world by now. They know we've lost our legions, our supply lines, our connection to the continent. If you were a Saxon war-chief, and you learned that the greatest empire in history had just collapsed, leaving a wealthy island defended by squabbling lords with no central command—would you raid for cattle? Or would you begin planning something larger?"

*Update your beliefs when you encounter new evidence*, Arthur thought. *The pattern fits invasion better than it fits raiding. The conclusion is uncomfortable, but comfort is not a guide to truth.*

Bedivere was silent for a long moment. His scars stood out white against his weathered face.

"I would send scouts," he said finally. "I would learn the land. I would count the fighting men and map the fortifications." His voice roughened. "And then I would come with every warrior I could muster, because an opportunity like this comes once in a hundred generations."

"Then we have perhaps a year. Maybe two, if we're fortunate and their harvest is good." Arthur moved back to the map. "I want a census of every fighting man in every holding that acknowledges the crown. I want an accounting of our grain stores, our forges, our horses. I want maps of every road, every river crossing, every defensible position between here and the coast."

"Your Majesty," Ector said carefully, "these are the preparations of a man expecting war."

"War is coming whether we expect it or not. I would rather be prepared than surprised."

---

The council moved to other matters—the mundane work of governance that filled most of Arthur's days.

"The tax collection from the northern holdings," announced Ector, consulting a wax tablet. "Lord Cador claims the harvest was poor and requests a reduction."

"Was the harvest poor?"

"Our observers report adequate yields, Your Majesty. Not abundant, but sufficient."

Arthur thought for a moment. "Send word to Lord Cador that his petition is noted. Also send word that the crown will be conducting its own assessment of the northern yields next month. If our findings differ from his claims..." He let the implication hang.

*People respond to incentives*, Arthur had learned. *Make lying costly, and they will tell fewer lies.*

"What else?"

"Master Aldric requests an audience regarding your studies, Your Majesty." This from Kay—Arthur's foster-brother, now serving as seneschal because he was the only person at court Arthur had known before the sword. "He says you missed yesterday's lesson."

Arthur suppressed a sigh. "I was reviewing the scouts' reports until dawn. Send Master Aldric my apologies and my request that we resume tomorrow."

"Which studies would those be, Your Majesty?" Bedivere's tone was carefully neutral, but Arthur caught the subtext. The boy-king, wasting time with books while the Saxons sharpen their axes.

"Latin, primarily. Also mathematics, natural philosophy, and what military history the library contains." Arthur met the older man's eyes. "The Roman legions conquered the known world, Lord Bedivere. They wrote down how they did it. If the Saxons attack, I intend to fight them with more than enthusiasm."

He had ordered the tutors himself, in the second week after the sword. The old king had apparently seen no need for a peasant king to be educated—or perhaps had assumed Arthur would be deposed before it mattered. But Arthur had discovered the fortress library, dusty and neglected, and had recognized it for what it was: a weapon no one else had thought to use.

*Vegetius on military tactics. Galen on medicine. Aristotle on reasoning—or at least, fragments of Aristotle, translated badly from Greek to Latin.* The Romans had preserved knowledge from across the ancient world. Most of it was useless now—who needed treatises on managing slaves when there were no slaves to manage?—but some of it was precious beyond measure.

*Knowledge does not care who learns it*, Arthur had realized, running his fingers over crumbling scrolls. *A peasant can read Aristotle's logic as well as a philosopher-king. The words do not change because of who speaks them.*

It was the same principle he had applied to the plague: observe, record, reason. The Romans had built an empire on systematic thinking. Perhaps their methods could save what remained of Britain.

"Anything else?" Arthur asked.

"A trade dispute between Lord Gareth and Lord Mervyn. Fishing rights on the river Usk."

"Send them both a copy of the Roman precedents on riparian boundaries. If they cannot agree based on established law, I will arbitrate in person—and neither will enjoy my decision."

Kay made a note. Brother Marcus shuffled forward with his own tablet.

"The Bishop sends his greetings, Your Majesty. And his concerns."

"His concerns about what?"

"The, ah, unusual nature of your council." Marcus glanced around the room—at Merlin's shadow by the pillar, at the absence of priests among the advisors, at the maps and census records that had replaced the chapel furniture. "The Bishop wonders if the crown might benefit from more... spiritual guidance."

*He wonders if a peasant-king is sufficiently pious*, Arthur translated. *He wonders whether I can be controlled.*

"Tell the Bishop that his guidance is always welcome. Tell him also that I pray daily—which is true—and that I have not forgotten the Church's role in crowning the kings of Britain." Arthur paused. "Tell him, finally, that I would be honored if he would visit the court to see for himself how I govern. Transparency builds trust."

Marcus bowed. His face was unreadable, but Arthur suspected the invitation would cause more alarm than comfort. *Let it. The Bishop can see that I am not raising demons or burning churches. What conclusions he draws from my preference for mathematics over prayer is his own affair.*

"Is there anything else urgent? Or can the remaining matters wait until tomorrow?"

The advisors exchanged glances. Ector shook his head. "Nothing that cannot wait, Your Majesty."

Arthur was about to dismiss the council when the doors of the throne room burst open.

---

A rider stood in the entrance, mud-spattered and gasping, still wearing the leather cap of a royal messenger. He had clearly ridden hard; his horse was probably dying in the courtyard.

"Your Majesty." The man's voice cracked. "Urgent news from the eastern coast."

Arthur felt a cold weight settle in his stomach. *The Saxons*, he thought—but no. Something about the messenger's face said this was worse than an early invasion.

"Speak."

"Camlann, Your Majesty. The village of Camlann." The messenger swallowed. "They received refugees from Londinium three weeks ago. Families fleeing the plague. The village elders took them in, fed them, gave them shelter."

*No.* Arthur's hands had gone cold before the messenger finished speaking.

"And?"

"The plague struck six days later. It spread so fast—" The messenger's voice broke. "By the time word reached us, half the village was dead. The survivors tried to flee to the neighboring holdings, but the local lord had heard what happened. He..." The man looked down. "He turned them away at spearpoint. The sick and the healthy alike. Said he would not let Camlann's fate become his own."

Arthur stared at the map on the council table. At the marks he had made for his way stations. At the neat, rational plan that would have saved Camlann if it had been implemented three weeks ago.

"How many?" he heard himself ask.

"Dead? Perhaps two hundred, Your Majesty. Those turned away—we don't know. They scattered into the countryside. Some will find shelter with distant kin. Others..." The messenger trailed off. "The local lord is asking for guidance. And for soldiers, if the refugees try to force their way past his borders."

The throne room was silent.

*Compassion*, Arthur had thought, planning his way stations. *Shelter and food and healers. The humane solution.*

But the humane solution required time. Required building way stations and staffing them and convincing the lords to cooperate. Weeks of preparation, at minimum. And the plague did not wait for preparation.

The lord who had turned the refugees away at spearpoint—he had not been humane. He had been *fast*. And his people were still alive.

For a moment, Arthur wanted nothing more than to scream. To rage at the unfairness of a world that punished compassion and rewarded cruelty. To demand answers from whatever power had placed a peasant boy on a throne and expected him to solve problems that had no good solutions.

Instead, he turned to the shadow by the pillar.

"You." His voice was flat, stripped of the careful neutrality he usually maintained. "Wizard. You've stood there for hours without speaking. You watched us plan for the plague. You heard the messenger. You have opinions on everything in this court, I'm told—everyone says so. Everyone says the great Merlin sees what others cannot." Arthur's hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against the table. "Tell me what I should have done. Tell me how I could have saved Camlann."

Merlin's expression didn't change. "You have capable advisors, Your Majesty. They do not need my counsel."

*Because you have none to give. Because everything you know is smoke and misdirection.*

"Then why do you attend these councils at all?"

"Because you have not yet dismissed me."

There was no insolence in the words. No challenge. Just a simple statement of fact, delivered with the patience of a man who had long since stopped caring what others thought of him.

Arthur studied the old man's face, looking for the tell—the flicker of calculation, the hidden smirk of a con artist watching his mark take the bait. He found nothing. Just weariness, and something else he couldn't name.

"I've been trying to understand you," Arthur said, lowering his voice so only Merlin could hear. "Six months since the sword, and I've watched you. You cast no spells. You heal no wounds. You offer no counsel that couldn't come from any village elder with a talent for obvious advice. The legendary wizard of Britain, and you're about as useful as a decorative tapestry."

Merlin said nothing.

"Except." Arthur's hand twitched toward the hilt of the sword at his belt—an unconscious gesture he'd developed since that day. His fingers brushed the cold metal of the pommel, and for an instant—

*Light. A pressure behind his eyes. The sense of something vast and patient, watching him from—*

He pulled his hand away. The feeling vanished, leaving only the familiar ache in his temples that came whenever he tried to remember what had happened when he drew the sword.

"Something happened when I pulled the blade," Arthur said quietly. "I remember reaching for it. I remember the stone yielding. I remember standing with the sword in my hand. But between reaching and holding—there's nothing. A gap. And there are moments when I know things I shouldn't know. When certainty settles into me like a weight, and I speak with an authority that feels *borrowed*."

He met Merlin's eyes.

"The Saxons will come in force within two years. I don't know how I know this. But I know it the way I know my own name."

Something flickered in Merlin's expression—there and gone too fast to read.

"You placed that sword in the stone," Arthur said. "You announced the test. You proclaimed whoever drew it would be king. And when a peasant boy pulled it free—convenient, wasn't it? A king with no allies, no family, no claim except your word. A boy who should be grateful. Who should listen to his elders."

"You believe I am manipulating you."

"I believe you placed a sword in a stone with the help of clever stonemasons. I believe you manufactured a prophecy to make yourself kingmaker. I believe you are a charlatan who has learned to counterfeit wisdom by speaking in riddles and watching for reactions."

Arthur leaned closer.

"What I cannot explain is *this*." He touched the sword's hilt again—lightly, briefly. "The knowing. The gap in my memory. The sense that something real happened, something that defies every rational explanation I can construct." He forced himself to hold Merlin's gaze. "So tell me, wizard: Is the sword magic, or is this another trick? Are you a fraud, or are you something else entirely?"

For a long moment, Merlin was silent. The torchlight flickered across his weathered face, making the shadows dance.

"Would you believe me," he said finally, "if I told you the answer?"

Arthur's jaw tightened. "Try me."

"No." Merlin's voice was flat. Final. "You are not ready."

Before Arthur could respond, the old wizard turned and walked toward the door. His walking staff clicked against the stone floor—slow, unhurried, as if two hundred people had not just died because of decisions made too late.

"Then why stay?" Arthur's voice cracked with frustration. "If you won't help—if you won't *explain*—why come to my councils at all? Why watch?"

Merlin paused at the threshold. He did not turn around.

"Because I have spent five hundred years watching civilizations rise and fall. Because I have seen a dozen kings make the choices you are learning to make." His voice was soft, but it carried in the silent hall. "And because, Your Majesty, for the first time in a very long time... I am not certain what will happen next."

Then he was gone, leaving Arthur alone with his advisors and his guilt and the weight of two hundred names he would never know.

---

The throne room fell silent in Merlin's wake.

Arthur turned back to the council table, to the map, to the marks he had made for way stations that would not be built in time. The other advisors watched him, waiting.

*This is what it means to rule*, Arthur thought. *Not choosing between good and evil. Choosing between evil and evil, and living with whichever one you pick.*

"The way stations proceed as planned," he said finally. His voice sounded distant, mechanical. "But until they are ready—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Until they are ready, any lord who wishes to close his borders to refugees from plague-struck areas has the crown's permission to do so."

Ector's eyebrows rose. "Your Majesty, moments ago you argued—"

"I argued for what *should* be. I am ordering what *must* be." Arthur could not look at the messenger. Could not think about the families turned away at spearpoint, wandering the roads with nowhere to go. "Draft the proclamation. Any lord who closes his borders must also provide supplies for those turned away—food enough to survive until the way stations are ready. That much, we can require."

"And if they refuse to provide supplies?"

"Then their borders remain open, and they can explain to their people why they chose pride over protection."

It was not enough. Arthur knew it was not enough. People would die because of this order—refugees who might have found shelter, villages that might have been saved if he had acted faster, thought harder, been *better*.

But people would die regardless. The only question was how many, and where, and whether Arthur could live with the answer.

He became aware that Merlin had returned.

The old wizard stood just inside the doorway, watching. His face was unreadable, but something in his posture had changed—a subtle tension, as if he were reevaluating something he had thought he understood.

Their eyes met.

Arthur expected mockery. Expected *I told you so* or *now you understand* or some other cryptic pronouncement dressed up as wisdom.

Merlin said nothing. He simply looked at Arthur for a long moment—looked at him the way you might look at someone carrying a weight you recognized—and then inclined his head, very slightly.

Not a bow. Not deference. Something else.

*Acknowledgment*, Arthur realized. *He's acknowledging that I just made an impossible choice and survived it.*

The old wizard walked away again, and this time he did not return.

Arthur watched him go.

For the first time, he did not think *charlatan*.

He thought: *He knows. Whatever this is—whatever I'm becoming—he knows.*

And he thought: *He's waiting for me to be ready to ask.*

The question was whether Arthur would ever be ready. Whether he could afford to be.

Behind him, the advisors were already arguing about supply lines and border protocols and the logistics of mercy constrained by mathematics. Arthur turned back to the map, to the work that never ended, to the weight of decisions that could not be unmade.

The sword hung heavy at his hip.

He did not touch it.

---

*End of Chapter One*
