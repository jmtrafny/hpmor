# The Weight of Atlantis

## Chapter Three: The Healer's Refusal

---

The child was dying, and Merlin was doing nothing.

Arthur stood in the doorway of what had once been a granary, now converted into something that had no proper name. Plague ward, perhaps. Death house. The smell hit him first—sickness and sweat and the sharp tang of herbs that did nothing but make the dying smell slightly better. Straw pallets lined the floor in rows. Thirty, maybe forty sick. More outside, waiting.

Brother Marcus moved between them with water and cloth. The monk's hands shook. He'd been here three days without rest.

"Your Majesty." Marcus didn't bow. Didn't have the strength for it. "You shouldn't be here."

"I'm already here." Arthur stepped inside. Boots on packed earth. Each footfall seemed too loud. "Show me."

Marcus led him down the row. Past a woman whose breathing rattled. Past an old man who'd stopped moving entirely. Past a girl, maybe six years old, whose parents knelt beside her pallet.

The girl's skin had the gray cast Arthur had learned to recognize. Fever, yes, but more than that. The plague had a look. A stillness that came before the end.

"Her name is Elspeth," Marcus said quietly. "She has hours, perhaps. Not days."

The mother was crying without sound. The father held her hand and stared at his daughter like he could will her to keep breathing.

Arthur had seen this before. Too many times. He'd ordered villages burned to stop the spread. He'd chosen which roads to close, which merchants to turn away, which families to isolate. Each decision had been correct. Each decision had killed someone.

The arithmetic was inescapable.

He turned. Merlin stood near the entrance, exactly where Arthur had left him. The advisor had said nothing since they'd arrived. His face was unreadable—that maddening stillness he wore like armor.

"Merlin." Arthur didn't raise his voice. Didn't need to. "Come here."

Merlin's eyes met his across the room. For a moment, Arthur thought he might refuse. Then the old man walked forward, steps careful, avoiding the pallets.

"Your Majesty?"

"Can you help her?"

It wasn't really a question. Arthur had brought Merlin here specifically to ask it. Three weeks since the terrible choice in the throne room. Three weeks of watching Merlin watch him, waiting for something Arthur couldn't name. Testing each other. Circling.

Arthur was done circling.

Merlin looked down at the dying child. His expression didn't change. "She is very ill."

"I can see that. Can you help her?"

"Magic is not—"

"Can you?" Arthur kept his voice level. Barely. "Yes or no."

The mother looked up. Hope flared in her eyes, desperate and painful. The father tensed.

Merlin was silent for three heartbeats. Four.

"Yes," he said finally. "I can."

Something in Arthur's chest unknotted and tightened simultaneously. "Then do it."

"Your Majesty—"

"Do it."

Another silence. Merlin looked at the child. At the parents. At Arthur.

Then he knelt.

His knees cracked on the hard floor. Old bones, old body. He reached out slowly and placed both hands on the girl's forehead.

The room held its breath.

For a moment, nothing happened. Then light bloomed between Merlin's palms and the child's skin. Soft, golden, like dawn breaking. It spread down from his hands, flowing across her face, her neck, her chest. The girl's body arched slightly. Her eyes flew open.

The mother gasped. The father made a sound like a sob.

The light grew brighter. Arthur could feel warmth radiating from it, pushing back the cold stench of death. Brother Marcus stumbled backward, making a sign against evil. Around the room, other plague victims were struggling to sit up, staring.

Merlin's face was drawn tight, concentrated. Sweat beaded on his forehead. The light pulsed once, twice—

And faded.

Merlin lifted his hands. The girl's skin was pink. Healthy. Her breathing came easy and deep. The gray cast was gone entirely.

The mother lunged forward, pulling her daughter into her arms. The father was weeping openly. The girl blinked, confused, and said, "Mama?"

Arthur stared. He'd seen Merlin's tricks before—the vanishing coin, the prophetic statements, the uncanny timing. But this. This was different. This was real. Magic was *real*, and Merlin—

From across the room, someone screamed.

"Help me!" An old woman on a pallet, arm outstretched. "Please, please help me!"

"My son!" A man near the back, cradling a boy. "He's worse than she was! Please!"

"Save us!"

"Please!"

"PLEASE!"

The chorus rose, desperate and ragged. Thirty voices, forty, all crying out. All begging. Brother Marcus looked stricken. The parents clutching Elspeth looked around in horror, realizing what they'd witnessed, what it meant.

Merlin stood slowly. He didn't look at the pleading crowd. He looked at Arthur.

"No," he said.

The word cut through the noise. Everyone fell silent.

Arthur's hands clenched. "What?"

"No." Merlin's voice was flat. Final. "I will not."

"You just—" Arthur gestured at the girl, healthy and breathing in her mother's arms. "You can save them. I saw you. Why won't you—"

"Because I cannot save them all." Merlin turned toward the door. "And choosing who lives would burden me beyond your understanding."

Arthur grabbed his arm. The old man stopped but didn't pull away.

"Then teach me," Arthur said. The words came fast, hot. "Teach others. We could train healers, establish—"

"No."

"You'd let them die? All of them? When you could—"

"I could do many things, Your Majesty." Merlin's eyes were ancient. Tired. "That does not mean I should."

He pulled his arm free gently and walked toward the entrance. The crowd parted for him, some shrinking back in fear, others reaching out in supplication. He ignored them all.

Arthur stood frozen. The girl was alive. Merlin could heal. The plague could be stopped. The death, the suffering, all of it could—

Could what? End? If Merlin healed them all? If he traveled village to village, touching the sick, bringing them back from the edge?

Why wouldn't he?

Arthur strode after him, past the crying sick, out into the cold morning air. Merlin was walking toward the horses, unhurried.

"That's all you have to say?" Arthur's voice cracked. He was fourteen years old and people were dying and his advisor could save them and wouldn't. The rage felt clean. Righteous. "No explanation? No reason?"

Merlin stopped. Turned. For the first time since Arthur had known him, genuine emotion flickered across his face. Sadness, perhaps. Or regret.

"Would you understand my reasons if I gave them?"

"Try me."

"No." Merlin's expression smoothed over again. "You are not ready."

He mounted his horse with surprising grace for an old man and rode away.

Arthur watched him go. Behind him, the plague house was erupting in fresh cries. Brother Marcus would try to calm them. The parents of the girl would be overwhelmed with questions. The village would be in chaos by nightfall.

One child saved. Forty left to die.

The arithmetic was inescapable.

---

The council meeting that afternoon was smaller than usual. Just Arthur, Bedivere, and Kay. The castle's chronicler sat in the corner, ready to record.

Arthur stood at the head of the table. He hadn't sat since returning from the village.

"I want documentation," he said. "Every time Merlin uses magic. When, where, who he helps, who he refuses. Every instance, every detail."

Kay frowned. "Your Majesty—"

"There's a pattern. There has to be. He healed one child but not the others. Why that child? Why that moment? What made her different?"

"Perhaps she wasn't as sick as the others," Bedivere offered carefully. "Perhaps the magic is limited, and she was the only one he could save."

"Then he would have said so." Arthur shook his head. "He said he chose not to. Which means there's a reason. A rule he's following."

"Or perhaps there is no rule." Bedivere's voice was gentle. Diplomatic. "My lord, investigating your own advisor—a man you appointed, a man who serves at your pleasure—it could undermine your authority. If people think you distrust him—"

"Being ignorant while people die already undermines it."

The words came out harder than Arthur intended. Bedivere fell silent.

Arthur forced himself to breathe. To think. "I'm not trying to undermine him. I'm trying to understand him. If Merlin has power—real power—and he's choosing not to use it, I need to know why. Maybe his reasons are good. Maybe they make sense. But I can't make decisions about my kingdom based on mysteries and riddles."

Kay nodded slowly. "You want us to watch him."

"I want us to document him. His patterns. Where he goes, what he does, when he intervenes and when he doesn't. If there's logic to it, we'll find it."

"And if there isn't?" Bedivere asked.

Arthur met his eyes. "Then we'll know that too."

The chronicler's pen scratched across parchment, recording the order. Arthur wondered if this would be seen as the moment he lost faith in his advisor. Or the moment he started taking his kingdom seriously.

Perhaps both were true.

"One more thing," Arthur said. "This stays quiet. No public announcements, no declarations. Just observation."

"Subtlety in matters of magic," Kay said dryly. "Nothing could go wrong there."

Arthur almost smiled. Almost. "Dismissed."

They left. Arthur stayed, staring at maps of the kingdom. The plague's spread marked in red. So many villages. So many sick.

One child saved.

One.

---

Evening found Arthur on the battlements. He came here when he needed to think. Wind and stone and sky. No advisors, no courtiers, no decisions that killed people.

Movement in the courtyard below caught his eye.

Merlin stood near the stables, alone. He was looking up.

Looking at Arthur.

Their eyes met across the distance. Sixty feet of empty air. The old man's face was shadowed, unreadable in the fading light. But he didn't look away. Didn't pretend he hadn't been watching.

Arthur didn't look away either.

Two people who knew exactly what the other was thinking. Arthur would investigate. Merlin would allow it. The game between them had shifted from subtle testing to open conflict.

A line had been drawn.

Merlin turned first, walking back toward his chambers. Arthur watched him go, then looked back at the maps in his mind. The plague spreading. The villages burning. The choices that would come tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that.

Merlin healed the child. Therefore, Merlin could heal. Therefore, every person who died while Merlin did nothing was a choice.

The arithmetic was inescapable.

Arthur would find the pattern. He would understand the rules. And then—

Then he would decide what to do about them.

The wind picked up, cold and sharp. Winter was coming. The plague would get worse before it got better.

If it got better.

Arthur turned from the battlements and went inside. There was work to do.

---

*End of Chapter Three*
